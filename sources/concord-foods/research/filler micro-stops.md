# Micro-stops on a syrup filling line: desk research for ILFA-2

Research checked 2026-11-13. Desk research only. It builds on `incident library.md` (same folder) and does not repeat its five case groups. Nothing here describes Concord's actual line, filler OEM, container or incident history. Every Concord-specific statement is a hypothesis until Aman confirms it on site.

**Access note.** Several trade-press and academic pages returned HTTP 403 or a bot wall: Packaging World, ProFood World, OEM Magazine, Packaging Digest, Healthcare Packaging, ResearchGate, the DUT repository and the Oklahoma State extension site. For those, this file relies on search-engine summaries of the page, marked **(snippet only)**. They were not read in full. They are useful leads, not verified evidence. Pages marked **(read)** were fetched and read as text. No video was watched.

## Answer up front

On lines that fill viscous products into plastic containers, published sources point to the same few short-stop families: containers down or jammed at a transfer into the filler or capper, cap feed and capping faults, product dripping or stringing onto the container and machine, and downstream blocking from case packing. A fallen or jammed empty container at the filler infeed is a recognised and camera-legible micro-stop. But almost every explicit "downed bottle" source is about small or slender bottles at high speed, not half-gallon handled jugs. No source found says handled jugs tip *more* often than other containers. Sources do show that handled jugs need special handling at starwheels and orienters, and that a syrup line has a distinct failure chain: a missing or loose cap lets sticky product escape, foul conveyors and trigger more stops. Recommendation (hypothesis): keep the infeed incident as the hero, but reframe it from "jug tipped" to "empty jug down or jammed at the filler infeed transfer", and add a capper or syrup-on-finish event as the second visible category.

## Handled-jug tipping

**What the sources support.**
- Empty plastic containers are top-heavy, and lightweighting raises the centre of gravity further. Falling containers cause jams and scrap ([US7708159](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/7708159), [US5159960](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/5159960), snippet only, A).
- Tall containers tend to fall on acceleration, deceleration and transfer between conveyors, and one fall can start a chain reaction ([US5555700](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/5555700), snippet only, A).
- For very light bottles one line chose pressureless conveying because the bottles "tip easily" ([Packaging Digest, aseptic line](https://www.packagingdigest.com/automation/bottles-fly-on-new-aseptic-line), snippet only, B).
- A one-gallon side-handled jug would not run on an existing filler starwheel without a redesign ([Packaging World, Ocean Spray](https://www.packworld.com/home/article/13335531/new-starwheel-avoids-filler-rebuild-at-ocean-spray), snippet only, B). Handled bottles also need orientation by vision or photoeye at rotary orienters ([Packaging World, Jim Beam](https://www.packworld.com/machinery/primary-packaging/article/13329190/new-level-of-packaging-technology-reaches-jim-beam-sidebar), snippet only, B).
- An empty one-gallon HDPE dairy jug weighs about 60 to 62 g; a lightweighted one 52 g ([Packaging World, dairy jugs](https://www.packworld.com:443/home/product/13366819/lightweighted-onegallon-dairy-jugs-conserve-hdpe-usage), snippet only, B). A half-gallon jug is lighter still [UNVERIFIED].

**What the sources do not support.**
- No source found says handled jugs tip more often than round bottles or cans. No source quantifies tipping frequency for any jug.
- The explicit downed-container sources are about small or slender containers: Fogg's reject covers 0.5 to 32 oz bottles ([Beverage Industry](https://www.bevindustry.com/articles/92717-fogg-filler-launches-new-down-bottle-reject-system), read, B). Septimatech's case concerns slender small-base containers (see `incident library.md`). Dewa's peer-reviewed conference paper found high filler fallen-bottle stoppages on a glass beer line ([DUT repository](https://ir.dut.ac.za/handle/10321/3958), snippet only, A).

**Hypothesis: conditions that would make an empty handled jug go down or jam.** (a) Light empty weight with a high, off-centre mass from the handle. (b) Speed changes and gaps closing at the infeed screw or starwheel. (c) Rail or change-part set for another format after changeover. (d) Line pressure in accumulation pushing jugs against each other at the handle. (e) Wet or sticky conveyor from syrup spill changing friction. Confirm by: site footage or operator recall of where down or jammed jugs occur, and whether a down-bottle reject exists. Kill if: operators say empty jugs almost never go down, or the line uses neck-handling or pucks.

**Hypothesis: comparison with cans.** Cans on a brewery line run at much higher speed, so falls there may be a speed effect rather than a shape effect. A broad-based half-gallon jug may be more stable when standing still but more prone to skewing and handle snagging at transfers. [UNVERIFIED] No source compares them. Confirm or kill by asking ILFA-2 operators which jam they clear most often at the filler infeed.

## Ranked recommendation (Hypothesis)

**Hypothesis:** the micro-stops most plausible for ILFA-2, ranked by fit with sources, syrup relevance and camera legibility. Prevalence on Concord's line is unknown.

| Rank | Micro-stop | Why | Camera? | Riskiest assumption (Mom Test question for Aman) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Empty jug down, skewed or jammed at the transfer into the filler (screw, starwheel or inline indexing gate) | Fallen containers at infeeds and transfer points are the most cited short stop. Handled jugs need special transfer parts | Yes, very legible | "When the filler last stopped for a container problem, where was the jug and what did you do?" |
| 2 | Capper fault: cocked, missing or cross-threaded cap, or torque reject | Named as a typical capper stop by several sources. Syrup adds sugar-in-clutch and product-on-finish mechanisms | Cocked or missing cap yes; torque and seal no | "Last week, how many times did the capper stop or reject, and what did you find on the jug?" |
| 3 | Syrup drip or string onto the jug neck, jug and conveyor, then cleanup | Stringing is a known viscous-product issue and feeds rank 2 and sticky conveyors | Yes at close range near the nozzle | "When did you last stop to wipe syrup off the filler or conveyor, and how long did it take?" |
| 4 | Missing or loose cap discovered at lay-down, with leak into the lay-down or cooler | Lay-down exposes missing caps. Maple syrup case shows sticky contamination | Yes | "Has a jug ever leaked in the lay-down or cooler? What happened next?" |
| 5 | Cap feed interruption (caps out or chute jam) | Named as a typical capper stop | Yes if the hopper or chute is in view | "Who refills the cap hopper, and when did it last run empty?" |

Fill-weight rejects from viscosity drift are probably real but are a data event, not a camera event. Keep them out of the hero role.

**Verdict on tipping.** Keep the infeed incident as the hero, but change it. Drop "jug tipped" as the framing and use "empty jug down or jammed at the filler infeed transfer". This covers tipping, skewing and handle snagging without asserting a cause. It stays inside what sources support and remains the most camera-legible event. Do not claim it is the most common stop on ILFA-2. If Aman's site evidence says jugs rarely go down, replace it with rank 2 (capper fault with syrup on the finish), which is the most syrup-specific chain found. Confirm on site: whether the filler is inline or rotary, whether a down-bottle reject exists and what operators clear most often.
